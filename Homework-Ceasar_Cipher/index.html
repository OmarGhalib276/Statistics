One message, two ciphers, two attacks.

This work looks at two very different ciphers applied to the same message: a toy public-key scheme built on the secp256k1 elliptic curve, whose private key is deliberately restricted to a small range, and the classical Caesar cipher. After comparing how they encrypt, how their keys work and what their ciphertexts reveal statistically, each one is attacked: the Caesar cipher through the statistics of the language, the toy scheme through its restricted key space.

Setup

1 · Toy ECC encryption and decryption

The curve is y² = x³ + 7 over the prime field of p = 2²⁵⁶ − 2³² − 977, with generator G of prime order n ≈ 1.16·10⁷⁷. The scheme is an ECIES-style hybrid: elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman gives a shared point, a hash turns it into a keystream, and the keystream is XORed with the message. The only “toy” ingredient is that the private key d is drawn from a small range.

2 · The Caesar cipher

Each letter is shifted k places in the alphabet: E(x) = (x + k) mod 26, D(y) = (y − k) mod 26. Case and punctuation are preserved. The key is a single shared number, so there are only 26 keys (25 useful).

4 · Statistical cryptanalysis of the Caesar cipher


A Caesar ciphertext has the letter distribution of the language shifted by k. The attacker learns that distribution from a corpus, then picks the shift that makes the candidate plaintext look most like the language. Three scoring rules are implemented:


  • Letter-frequency analysis: choose k minimising χ² = Σ (Oᵢ − Eᵢ)² / Eᵢ between the candidate’s letter counts and the corpus frequencies.
    • 

  • n-gram scoring: choose k maximising the log-likelihood Σ log P(xᵢ | xᵢ₋₂xᵢ₋₁) under a trigram model learned from the corpus.
    • 

  • Exhaustive search with a crib: try all 26 shifts and keep the one containing the most common fragments (the, and, ing, ion, ent, for, tha). It is a dictionary test rather than statistics.

Effectiveness and limitations


  • Length drives success. With a few letters the histogram is mostly noise and several shifts look equally plausible; as the length grows the correct shift stands out. Read the thresholds from the table above.
    • 

  • The model matters. Trigrams use more structure per letter than single-letter counts, so they usually need less text.
    • 

  • Corpus mismatch hurts. A corpus in another language, era or style shifts the reference distribution and degrades every method.
    • 

  • Why it works at all. The cipher preserves the statistics of the plaintext. With only 25 non-trivial keys, even blind exhaustive search is instant; the statistical method matters as a template for larger monoalphabetic ciphers.



5 · Attacking the restricted-key ECC scheme


An eavesdropper sees Q, R and c. Since d ∈ [1, 2^b], solving Q = dG (the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem on a tiny instance) recovers d, after which the decryption is the honest one. Two algorithms are implemented:


  • Exhaustive search: compute G, 2G, 3G, … by repeated addition until the point equals Q. About 2^b steps, negligible memory.
    • 

  • Baby-step giant-step (BSGS): write d = i·m + j with m = ⌈√(2^b)⌉. Store the baby steps jG in a table, then walk Q − i·mG until a point is in the table. About 2^(b/2) steps and 2^(b/2) memory.




Run section 1 first. Exhaustive search above about 22 bits can take minutes.

Why it fails against a properly configured key


A real secp256k1 private key is uniform below n ≈ 2²⁵⁶. Exhaustive search needs about 2²⁵⁵ steps; the best generic algorithms (Pollard’s rho, BSGS) need about √n ≈ 2¹²⁸ group operations, and BSGS would also need 2¹²⁸ stored points. No known algorithm does substantially better on this curve. The ciphertext offers no statistical shortcut either, since it looks like random bytes.

6 · Summary


  • The toy scheme decrypts correctly because rQ = dR; d is the long-lived secret, Q is public and r is one-time.
    • 

  • Caesar and the toy scheme are both additive stream-like ciphers without diffusion or integrity, but their statistics are opposite: Caesar keeps the fingerprint of the language, the ECC output looks uniform.
    • 

  • Caesar falls to statistics, with reliability growing with ciphertext length; the restricted-key ECC scheme falls to key search, independently of the message.
    • 

  • Larger keys defeat the second attack, but the design flaws (nonce reuse, no authentication) would survive.
